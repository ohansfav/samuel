3.1 INTRODUCTION TO SYSTEM ANALYSIS
------------------------------------

System analysis is the process of studying a problem domain with the aim of
recommending improvements and specifying the requirements of a new system. It
involves identifying the problem, understanding user requirements, gathering
data, analysing the existing system, and proposing a solution that satisfies
the identified needs.

For this project — a Blockchain-Based Voting System — system analysis was
undertaken to thoroughly understand the shortcomings of traditional and
existing electronic voting systems, and to establish the technical and
functional requirements that a blockchain-based solution must fulfil. The
analysis covered three major dimensions:

  (i)  Organisational Analysis: Understanding the stakeholders involved in an
       election process — voters, electoral commission officials, candidates,
       and independent observers.

  (ii) Technical Analysis: Evaluating the technology stack suitable for
       implementing a decentralised, tamper-proof voting infrastructure,
       including smart contracts, Web3 wallet integration, and relay
       infrastructure.

  (iii) Security Analysis: Assessing threat models relevant to digital voting,
        including double voting, identity spoofing, vote tampering, replay
        attacks, and denial-of-service scenarios.

System analysis in this project followed the Structured Systems Analysis and
Design Method (SSADM), complemented by object-oriented analysis principles.
Tools used during the analysis phase include data flow diagrams, use-case
diagrams, entity-relationship models, and Unified Modelling Language (UML)
notation.

The outcome of the system analysis phase defined the scope of the proposed
system, identified the data entities and their relationships, established the
functional and non-functional requirements, and provided the blueprint upon
which the system design and implementation were built.

The analysis confirmed that blockchain technology — specifically Ethereum smart
contracts — provides the cryptographic guarantees, decentralisation, and audit
trail capabilities necessary for a trustworthy digital voting environment.

3.2 RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
--------------------------

The research methodology adopted for this project is a mixed-method, applied
research approach, combining qualitative inquiry with technical development and
experimental validation.

3.2.1 Research Design

An applied research design was selected because the primary objective of this
work is to produce a functional software artefact (the blockchain voting
system) that addresses a real-world problem. The design incorporates the
following phases:

  Phase 1 — Literature Review:
    A systematic review of existing literature on electronic voting systems,
    blockchain technology, smart contracts, and decentralised application (DApp)
    development was conducted. Key sources included IEEE journals, ACM Digital
    Library, Ethereum documentation, OpenZeppelin contract libraries, and prior
    academic projects on e-voting.

  Phase 2 — Requirements Elicitation:
    Qualitative interviews and structured questionnaires were used to elicit
    requirements from potential stakeholders. The target groups included
    students (as potential voters), academic staff (as election administrators),
    and software engineers familiar with blockchain development.

  Phase 3 — System Design and Development:
    An iterative, prototype-based development approach was used. Designs were
    implemented in short cycles, tested, reviewed, and refined. The Hardhat
    development environment was used for smart contract compilation, testing,
    and deployment.

  Phase 4 — System Testing and Evaluation:
    The system was tested using both automated unit tests (Hardhat test suite)
    and manual end-to-end testing on the Ethereum Sepolia testnet and a local
    Hardhat blockchain node.

  Phase 5 — Analysis and Documentation:
    Results from testing were analysed and documented. Performance metrics,
    security assessments, and functional correctness were evaluated against
    predefined criteria.

3.2.2 Research Approach

The research adopts a deductive approach, starting from known blockchain
principles and cryptographic standards (ECDSA signatures, EIP-712 typed data,
ERC-2771 meta-transactions) and applying them to the specific domain of
digital voting. Hypotheses derived from the literature — that blockchain can
provide immutability, transparency, and verifiable anonymity — were tested
through system implementation and empirical evaluation.

3.2.3 Data Collection Instruments

Primary data was collected via:
  - Online questionnaires administered to prospective users
  - Structured observation of user interactions with the system prototype
  - Transaction logs and smart contract events captured on the blockchain

Secondary data was gathered from:
  - Academic journals and conference papers on blockchain voting
  - Ethereum Improvement Proposals (EIPs) — specifically EIP-712 and EIP-2771
  - OpenZeppelin smart contract audits and security advisories

3.2.4 Ethical Considerations

All participants in questionnaires and user-testing sessions provided informed
consent. No personally identifiable information was collected beyond what is
inherent in blockchain addresses (which are pseudonymous). The study adhered
to the institution's ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects.

3.3 DESCRIPTION OF THE EXISTING SYSTEM
-----------------------------------------

3.3.1 Overview of Traditional Voting Systems

Traditional voting systems — whether paper-based or conventional electronic
voting machine (EVM) based — have been in use for decades in Nigeria and
across the world. While they have facilitated democratic processes, they are
fraught with significant limitations that undermine public trust in election
outcomes.

3.3.2 Paper-Based Voting (Manual System)

In paper-based voting, voters physically present themselves at polling stations,
are verified against a voters' register, and cast votes by marking a ballot
paper. The ballots are manually counted by returning officers.

Identified Weaknesses:
  (a) Vote Rigging and Manipulation: Ballot boxes can be physically snatched,
      stuffed with pre-marked ballots, or have results altered at counting
      centres. Nigeria's electoral history documents numerous instances of this.

  (b) Multiple Voting: Without robust real-time verification, a voter may
      appear at multiple polling units with forged accreditation cards.

  (c) Transparency Deficit: The manual counting process is observable only to
      party agents physically present. Independent observers lack access to the
      raw data.

  (d) Human Error: Manual counting introduces counting errors, especially in
      large-scale elections with millions of votes.

  (e) Logistical Challenges: Distributing ballot materials across large
      geographies, especially in rural Nigeria, is expensive and time-consuming.
      Results take days or weeks to tally.

  (f) Accessibility: Voters with physical disabilities, those resident abroad,
      or those unable to travel face significant barriers to participation.

3.3.3 Conventional Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs)

Some jurisdictions have adopted standalone electronic voting machines. These
systems digitise the ballot-marking process but retain several vulnerabilities.

Identified Weaknesses:
  (a) Centralised Data Storage: Vote tallies are stored on the EVM's internal
      memory or transmitted to a central server, creating a single point of
      failure and a target for manipulation.

  (b) Software Vulnerabilities: EVMs run proprietary software that is not
      publicly auditable. Independent researchers have demonstrated
      vulnerabilities in several commercially deployed EVMs.

  (c) Physical Tampering: The devices themselves can be subjected to hardware
      attacks if not secured properly throughout the electoral process.

  (d) Auditability: Once an election is concluded, verifying individual votes
      is often impossible without access to proprietary vendor software.

  (e) Trust in Central Authority: The integrity of the system depends entirely
      on trusting the electoral management body, the EVM vendor, and network
      administrators — a trust that is frequently disputed.

3.3.4 Early E-Voting and Internet Voting Systems

Some institutions and jurisdictions have experimented with web-based voting
portals. These systems allow remote voting via a browser or mobile application.

Identified Weaknesses:
  (a) Server-Side Manipulation: Votes are stored in centralised databases that
      can be altered by administrators or compromised by external attackers.

  (b) Man-in-the-Middle Attacks: Votes transmitted over networks can be
      intercepted and altered if not protected with end-to-end encryption.

  (c) Authentication Weakness: Password-based authentication is susceptible to
      credential theft, phishing, and social engineering.

  (d) Denial of Service: Centralised servers can be overwhelmed by deliberate
      DDoS attacks timed to disrupt voting.

  (e) No Immutable Audit Trail: Database records can be deleted or modified
      without any cryptographic proof of tampering.

3.3.5 Summary of Existing System Deficiencies

The following table summarises the critical weaknesses of existing voting systems:

+-------------------------+----------+-----------+------------------+
| Deficiency              | Paper    | EVM-Based | Web-Based Portal |
+-------------------------+----------+-----------+------------------+
| Vote tampering risk     | High     | Medium    | High             |
| Double voting risk      | High     | Low       | Medium           |
| Transparency            | Low      | Low       | Low              |
| Audit trail             | Partial  | Limited   | No               |
| Central point of failure| Yes      | Yes       | Yes              |
| Accessibility           | Low      | Medium    | High             |
| Real-time results       | No       | Partial   | Yes              |
| Public verifiability    | No       | No        | No               |
+-------------------------+----------+-----------+------------------+

3.4 THE PROPOSED SYSTEM
-------------------------

3.4.1 Overview

The proposed system is a Blockchain-Based Voting System implemented as a
decentralised web application (DApp) running on the Ethereum blockchain network.
The system leverages the inherent properties of blockchain technology —
immutability, decentralisation, cryptographic security, and transparency — to
address the documented shortcomings of existing voting systems.

The system comprises three primary architectural tiers:

  Tier 1 — Frontend Application:
    A React.js single-page application (SPA) that provides the voter interface.
    Voters interact via a web browser with MetaMask wallet extension installed.
    The frontend handles wallet connection, candidate display, vote submission,
    real-time status updates, and result visualisation.

  Tier 2 — Relay Infrastructure (Meta-Transaction Layer):
    An Express.js Node.js server (the "relayer") that sponsors gas fees on
    behalf of voters using ERC-2771 meta-transaction architecture. This ensures
    voters can cast votes without holding Ether, removing a significant barrier
    to adoption.

  Tier 3 — Blockchain Layer (Smart Contracts):
    Two Solidity smart contracts deployed on Ethereum:
      - Voting.sol: The core election contract that records candidates and votes
      - MinimalForwarder.sol: The ERC-2771 forwarder that validates and relays
        sponsored transactions

3.4.2 Key Features of the Proposed System

  (a) Immutable Vote Recording: Once a vote is cast and confirmed on the
      Ethereum blockchain, it is mathematically impossible to alter or delete it
      without invalidating the entire chain — a computational feat beyond any
      practical adversary.

  (b) Double-Vote Prevention: The smart contract maintains an on-chain mapping
      (mapping(address => bool) public voters) that permanently records which
      wallet addresses have voted. Any attempt to vote a second time from the
      same address is rejected by the contract at the EVM level.

  (c) Cryptographic Authentication: Voters authenticate using their Ethereum
      wallet (MetaMask). Each vote is cryptographically signed using ECDSA with
      the voter's private key, providing mathematically verifiable proof of
      authorship.

  (d) Sponsored (Gas-Free) Transactions: The meta-transaction relay server
      enables voters to cast votes without paying Ethereum gas fees. The system
      operator's wallet covers gas costs, democratising participation.

  (e) Public Audit Trail: All transactions are recorded on the public Ethereum
      blockchain. Any independent party can verify the election results by
      querying the blockchain explorer (e.g., Sepolia Etherscan).

  (f) Time-Bounded Voting: The smart contract enforces voting periods through
      block timestamps. Voting is only possible between votingStart and
      votingEnd as set in the contract constructor, eliminating late votes.

  (g) Administrator Controls: The contract owner (election administrator) can
      add candidates. The administrator cannot alter or delete votes, ensuring
      no privileged manipulation.

  (h) Demo/Local Mode: The system supports a local Hardhat blockchain mode for
      demonstration purposes, enabling zero-gas presentations without internet
      or testnet funds.

3.4.3 System Workflow

  Step 1: Voter opens the web application in a browser with MetaMask installed.
  Step 2: Voter clicks "Connect Wallet" — MetaMask requests account access.
  Step 3: The system verifies the connected network matches the target chain.
  Step 4: The smart contract is queried for candidates, voting status, and
          whether the voter has already voted.
  Step 5: The voter selects their preferred candidate and clicks "Vote".
  Step 6: If the relayer URL is configured, the vote is routed through the
          meta-transaction path (gas-free). Otherwise, the voter signs a
          direct transaction.
  Step 7: The relayer server verifies the EIP-712 signature, submits the
          transaction to the blockchain, and returns the transaction hash.
  Step 8: The smart contract executes, increments the candidate's vote count,
          and marks the voter's address as having voted.
  Step 9: The frontend polls the contract and updates the live results display.

3.5 SIGNIFICANCE AND CHALLENGES OF THE PROPOSED SYSTEM
---------------------------------------------------------

3.5.1 Significance

The significance of this blockchain-based voting system extends beyond academic
exercise. It addresses a critical national challenge — election integrity —
using proven, globally adopted technology. Key areas of significance include:

  (a) Electoral Integrity:
    The immutable, cryptographically secured vote ledger eliminates the primary
    mechanism through which electoral fraud occurs: post-facto alteration of
    vote tallies. Once data is on-chain, no single entity — not even the system
    developer — can change it.

  (b) Public Trust in Democratic Institutions:
    Transparent, publicly verifiable election results directly address the
    credibility crisis that plagues many democratic institutions. Voters and
    observers can independently verify outcomes without relying on official
    declarations.

  (c) Cost Reduction:
    Digital voting eliminates the material costs of paper ballots, printing,
    physical distribution logistics, and manual counting operations. In a
    national election context, these savings are substantial.

  (d) Increased Participation:
    By enabling remote, internet-based voting with a simple browser and wallet,
    the system removes geographic barriers to participation, potentially
    increasing voter turnout among the youth and diaspora.

  (e) Academic Contribution:
    This project demonstrates the practical applicability of blockchain
    technology to governance, providing a reference implementation that can be
    studied, extended, or adapted for institutional elections, student union
    elections, and referendums.

  (f) Alignment with Global Trends:
    Governments and organisations worldwide are exploring blockchain voting. This
    project positions the implementing institution at the forefront of this
    emerging field and provides hands-on experience in Web3 development.

3.5.2 Challenges of the Proposed System

Despite its significant advantages, the proposed system faces a number of
technical, social, and operational challenges:

  (a) Digital Divide:
    A requirement to use a computer or smartphone with MetaMask browser
    extension excludes voters without digital literacy or internet access,
    particularly in rural and underserved communities. This remains the most
    significant adoption barrier.

  (b) Wallet Key Management:
    The security of a voter's vote depends entirely on the security of their
    private key. Loss or theft of the private key (or seed phrase) compromises
    the voter's identity on the blockchain. Unlike passwords, lost private keys
    cannot be recovered.

  (c) Scalability Limitations:
    Ethereum's mainnet processes approximately 15-30 transactions per second.
    A national election with millions of simultaneous voters would overwhelm
    Ethereum's base layer. Layer-2 scaling solutions (Optimism, Arbitrum) or
    alternative consensus mechanisms would be required at national scale.

  (d) Gas Costs (Without Relayer):
    Without the meta-transaction relay infrastructure, every voter must hold
    Ether to pay gas fees, which is impractical for mass adoption.

  (e) Smart Contract Bugs:
    Smart contracts are immutable once deployed. A bug in the Voting.sol
    contract — particularly in vote counting or access control — cannot be
    patched without deploying an entirely new contract, potentially
    invalidating ongoing elections.

  (f) Regulatory and Legal Framework:
    Most jurisdictions lack a legal framework that recognises blockchain-based
    electoral results as legally binding. Adoption requires legislative action.

  (g) User Experience Complexity:
    The requirement to install MetaMask, manage an Ethereum account, and
    understand network switching introduces friction that typical voters
    accustomed to paper ballots may find prohibitive.

  (h) Network Dependency:
    Unlike a physical ballot box, the system requires a reliable internet
    connection at the time of voting. Network outages — whether natural or
    deliberately induced — could prevent votes from being submitted.

3.7 DATA SET COLLECTION
-------------------------

3.7.1 Overview of Data Collection Strategy

The blockchain-based voting system employs a multi-layered data collection
approach that captures voter identity, voting preferences, and transactional
metadata across three primary tiers: client-side (frontend), relay
infrastructure, and the immutable blockchain layer. This distributed
architecture ensures data integrity while maintaining voter privacy through
cryptographic protocols and decentralised storage mechanisms.

3.7.2 Data Sources and Collection Points

3.7.2.1 Client-Side Data Collection (Frontend Layer)

The frontend application (React-based) collects data from voters at the point
of interaction.

Voter Authentication Data:
  - Wallet Address: Collected via MetaMask Web3 integration when a voter
    connects their wallet. Implementation uses ethers.js Web3Provider:

      const provider = new ethers.providers.Web3Provider(window.ethereum);
      await provider.send('eth_requestAccounts', []);
      const signer = provider.getSigner();
      const address = await signer.getAddress();

  - Network Information: Chain ID, network name (e.g., Sepolia), and RPC
    endpoint verification are captured via eth_chainId.

  - Session Data: Connection timestamps and wallet connection status are held
    in React state (useState hooks) for the duration of the browser session.

Voting Data:
  - Candidate Selection: Captured when voter selects their preferred candidate
    (integer index: 0 to n-1)
  - Voting Status: Boolean indicator of whether the voter has already cast a vote
  - Vote Confirmation: Transaction hash and timestamp of vote submission

3.7.2.2 Smart Contract Data Collection (Blockchain Layer)

The Voting.sol smart contract automatically collects and immutably records
voting data on-chain.

Collected Data Elements:

  struct Candidate {
      string name;        // Candidate identifier
      uint256 voteCount;  // Cumulative vote tally
  }
  mapping(address => bool) public voters;  // Voter participation register

Data Captured at Vote Time:
  - Voter Address (msg.sender or meta-tx sender): Cryptographically derived
    from the voter's EIP-712 signature
  - Candidate Index: Integer index identifying the selected candidate
  - Block Timestamp: Exact time of vote execution (block.timestamp)
  - Transaction Hash: Unique 32-byte identifier for the voting transaction
  - Nonce Value: Sequential counter from MinimalForwarder preventing replays

Contract State Variables:
  - votingStart (uint256): Election commencement timestamp (Unix epoch)
  - votingEnd (uint256): Election closure timestamp (Unix epoch)
  - trustedForwarder (address): Relay infrastructure identifier
  - owner (address): Election administrator address

3.7.2.3 Relayer Infrastructure Data Collection

The relayer server (relayer/server.js) collects and processes meta-transaction
data via two HTTP endpoints:

  POST /meta-tx/prepare — Receives:
    - voter: Ethereum address of the voter
    - candidateIndex: Selected candidate (non-negative integer)
    - Returns: nonce, domain, request structure for EIP-712 signing

  POST /meta-tx/submit — Receives:
    - request: Complete ForwardRequest struct
    - signature: EIP-712 encoded voter signature
    - Returns: transaction hash and block confirmation

Meta-Transaction Request Structure:
  {
    from:  <voter address>,
    to:    <Voting contract address>,
    value: 0,
    gas:   300000,
    nonce: <sequential nonce from MinimalForwarder>,
    data:  <ABI-encoded vote(candidateIndex) call>
  }

EIP-712 Domain Context:
  {
    name:              "MinimalForwarder",
    version:           "0.0.1",
    chainId:           <target chain ID>,
    verifyingContract: <MinimalForwarder address>
  }

3.7.6 Data Storage Mechanisms

Blockchain Storage (Immutable, On-Chain):
  mapping(address => bool) public voters;     // Voter participation register
  Candidate[] public candidates;              // Array of candidate structs

  Characteristics:
    - Immutability: Once written, data cannot be altered or deleted
    - Transparency: All state changes are publicly auditable
    - Persistence: Survives network restarts and system failures
    - Cost: Approximately 20,000 gas per SSTORE operation

Temporary Storage (Frontend Session — React State):
  const [account, setAccount]         = useState(null);
  const [candidates, setCandidates]   = useState([]);
  const [hasVoted, setHasVoted]       = useState(false);
  const [activityFeed, setActivityFeed] = useState([]);

  Characteristics:
    - Ephemeral: Lost on page refresh or browser close
    - Client-side: Not transmitted to any server
    - Privacy: No server-side logging of voter actions

Browser Local Storage (Demo Mode):
  const storageKey = 'demo_voter_address';
  window.localStorage.setItem(storageKey, demoAddress);

  Purpose: Maintains persistent voter identity across browser sessions for
  local demonstration and testing without MetaMask.

3.7.7 Data Collection Sequence of Events

  Event 1 — Connection (T0):
    Voter clicks "Connect Wallet"
    Data collected: Wallet address via eth_requestAccounts

  Event 2 — Network Verification (T1):
    System calls eth_chainId
    Data collected: Current chain ID; switched to target if mismatched

  Event 3 — Contract Queries (T2):
    Parallel queries: candidates, votingStatus, remainingTime, voterStatus
    Data collected: Complete election state from smart contract

  Event 4 — Vote Selection (T3):
    Voter clicks candidate button
    Data collected: candidateIndex (0-indexed integer)

  Event 5 — Signature Request (T4):
    MetaMask presents EIP-712 typed data for signing
    Data collected: ECDSA signature authorising the vote transaction

  Event 6 — Transaction Submission (T5):
    Direct or relayed transaction submitted to blockchain
    Data collected: Transaction hash, block number, on-chain vote record

3.7.8 Data Privacy and Security Measures

  - ECDSA Signatures: All votes cryptographically signed by voter private key
  - EIP-712 Standard: Structured data hashing prevents signature misuse
    across different contracts or domains
  - Nonce Protection: MinimalForwarder's sequential nonce counter prevents
    transaction replay attacks
  - Pseudonymity: Wallet addresses are public but not directly linked to
    real-world identity, preserving voter anonymity
  - No PII Storage: The system never stores names, national identification
    numbers, or contact details on-chain or off-chain
  - Client-Side Only: Frontend data never leaves the user's browser to a
    centralised server (except for the meta-transaction payload)

3.8 SYSTEM DESIGN
------------------

3.8.1 Structural Design

The system is structured as a three-tier decentralised application:

  Presentation Tier — React Frontend (src/):
    - App.js: Root application component managing global state and event logic
    - Components/Login.jsx: Wallet connection and pre-voting interface
    - Components/Connected.jsx: Active voting interface with candidate display
    - Components/Finished.jsx: Post-voting results and election closure view
    - Components/RavenBackground.jsx: Visual background component
    - Constant/constant.js: Contract address and ABI configuration

  Business Logic Tier — Smart Contracts (contracts/):
    - Voting.sol: Core election logic (candidate management, vote casting,
      time enforcement, double-vote prevention)
    - MinimalForwarder.sol: ERC-2771 meta-transaction forwarder for gas
      sponsorship

  Relay/Middleware Tier — Node.js Server (relayer/):
    - server.js: Production relayer for Sepolia testnet
    - mock-server.js: Development mock server for local testing
    - local-chain-server.js: In-process Hardhat blockchain for demos

  Data Tier — Ethereum Blockchain:
    - Immutable storage of all votes, candidate data, and election parameters
    - Sepolia testnet (production testing) or Hardhat local node (development)

3.8.2 Database Design and Specification

Unlike traditional systems, this application does not use a relational database.
All persistent data is stored in smart contract state on the Ethereum blockchain.
The data model is defined by Solidity state variables:

  Entity: Candidate
    - name (string): Human-readable candidate name
    - voteCount (uint256): Accumulated vote total

  Entity: Voter
    - address (implicit key): Ethereum wallet address
    - hasVoted (bool): Participation status

  Entity: Election Configuration
    - votingStart (uint256): Start timestamp (Unix seconds)
    - votingEnd (uint256): End timestamp (Unix seconds)
    - owner (address): Election administrator
    - trustedForwarder (address): ERC-2771 forwarder contract address

  Relationships:
    - One Election has many Candidates (Candidate[] array)
    - One Election has many Voters (address => bool mapping)
    - One Voter casts at most one Vote to exactly one Candidate

  Data Integrity Constraints (enforced by EVM):
    - voters[address] == false required before casting a vote
    - candidateIndex must be less than candidates.length
    - Current block.timestamp must be between votingStart and votingEnd
    - Only owner may call addCandidate()

3.8.3 Interface Design

The user interface was designed following principles of simplicity,
accessibility, and clear feedback.

Login Screen (Login.jsx):
  - Prominent "Connect Wallet" button as the primary call-to-action
  - Status message area providing real-time feedback on connection state
  - Clear network indicator showing target blockchain and current chain
  - Error display for MetaMask unavailability or wrong network

Active Voting Screen (Connected.jsx):
  - Grid display of candidate cards, each showing candidate name and
    current vote count
  - Vote selection interface with confirmation dialog before submission
  - Transaction phase indicator (idle / signing / submitting / confirmed)
  - Real-time countdown timer showing remaining voting time
  - Activity feed showing recent blockchain events
  - Total votes counter updating in real-time
  - Explorer link allowing users to verify their transaction on Etherscan

Results Screen (Finished.jsx):
  - Final tally for all candidates displayed in ranked order
  - Visual representation of vote distribution
  - Election closed status with final timestamp

3.10.2 Component Interaction

  Frontend ↔ MetaMask:
    Via the window.ethereum EIP-1193 provider API injected by MetaMask.
    Communication is synchronous request-response (JSON-RPC).

  Frontend ↔ Smart Contract (Direct):
    Via ethers.js Contract instance. Read operations use call() (free);
    write operations use sendTransaction() requiring gas.

  Frontend ↔ Relayer (Sponsored):
    Via HTTP POST requests (fetch API) to the relayer Express server.
    The frontend sends the voter address and candidate index; the relayer
    prepares and executes the meta-transaction.

  Relayer ↔ MinimalForwarder:
    Via ethers.js. The relayer verifies the EIP-712 signature using
    forwarder.verify() then executes with forwarder.execute().

  MinimalForwarder ↔ Voting.sol:
    ERC-2771 internal call. The forwarder appends the voter address to the
    calldata; Voting.sol uses ERC2771Context._msgSender() to recover it.

3.10.3 Security Architecture

  Authentication Layer:
    Cryptographic — ECDSA secp256k1 signature verification. No username/
    password system. Identity is proven by control of private key.

  Authorisation Layer:
    Smart contract access control via onlyOwner modifier. All other functions
    are permissionless (any address may vote once).

  Integrity Layer:
    EVM determinism ensures all nodes compute identical state. Transaction
    ordering is enforced by block miners. Immutability is guaranteed by the
    cryptographic hash chain.

  Replay Protection:
    MinimalForwarder maintains a per-address nonce. The contract rejects any
    forwarded transaction with a nonce already consumed.

  Domain Separation:
    EIP-712 domain parameters (contract address + chain ID) prevent signatures
    obtained for this system from being replayed on other contracts or chains.

3.12 PERFORMANCE EVALUATION METRICS
--------------------------------------

The following metrics were defined to evaluate the performance and correctness
of the blockchain-based voting system:

3.12.1 Functional Correctness Metrics

  (a) Vote Recording Accuracy:
    Definition: Percentage of submitted votes correctly recorded on-chain.
    Target: 100% (any failure to record a valid vote is a critical defect)
    Measurement: Compare submitted votes (from relayer logs) vs. on-chain
    events emitted by the Voting contract.

  (b) Double-Vote Prevention Rate:
    Definition: Percentage of second-vote attempts correctly rejected.
    Target: 100% (zero tolerance for double voting)
    Measurement: Submit votes from the same address twice; confirm the second
    reverts with "You have already voted."

  (c) Time Boundary Enforcement:
    Definition: System correctly rejects votes before votingStart and after
    votingEnd.
    Target: 100%
    Measurement: Attempt votes at boundary timestamps in unit tests.

3.12.2 Performance Metrics

  (a) Transaction Confirmation Time:
    Definition: Time between vote submission and on-chain confirmation
    (minimum 1 block confirmation).
    Metric: Average block time on Sepolia (~12 seconds per block).

  (b) Frontend Load Time:
    Definition: Time from page load to full candidate list display.
    Target: Under 5 seconds on standard broadband connection.

  (c) Relayer Response Time:
    Definition: Time taken by the relayer to prepare and submit a
    meta-transaction.
    Measurement: Timestamp POST request arrival and blockchain submission.
    Target: Under 15 seconds including confirmation.

3.12.3 Security Metrics

  (a) Replay Attack Resistance:
    Verified by attempting to resubmit a captured EIP-712 signature.
    Expected result: MinimalForwarder rejects the replayed nonce.

  (b) Signature Validity Enforcement:
    Verified by submitting malformed or forged signatures.
    Expected result: forwarder.verify() returns false; transaction rejected.

  (c) Access Control:
    Verified by calling addCandidate() from a non-owner address.
    Expected result: EVM reverts with "Only owner."

3.12.4 Gas Consumption Metrics

  +----------------------------+--------------------+-------------------+
  | Operation                  | Estimated Gas      | ETH Cost (approx) |
  +----------------------------+--------------------+-------------------+
  | Deploy Voting.sol          | ~1,200,000 gas     | ~0.024 ETH        |
  | Deploy MinimalForwarder    | ~800,000 gas       | ~0.016 ETH        |

4.2 LANGUAGE IMPLEMENTATION
-----------------------------

The implementation of the blockchain voting system spans three distinct
programming environments, each serving a defined architectural role:

4.2.1 Solidity (Smart Contract Implementation)

Solidity was used to implement the on-chain business logic. The contracts
directory contains two core smart contracts:

  contracts/Voting.sol:
    The primary election contract. Implements candidate storage (a dynamic
    Candidate[] array), the double-vote prevention mapping, time-bounded
    voting enforcement via block timestamps, the vote() function, and
    administrative functions (addCandidate()). Extends OpenZeppelin's
    ERC2771Context to support gas-free meta-transactions.

    Key implementation decisions:
      - The use of uint256 for all numeric values (vote counts, timestamps)
        avoids signed-integer underflow vulnerabilities.
      - The onlyOwner modifier uses require() for access control rather than
        a custom error, maintaining compatibility with older Solidity tooling.
      - _msgSender() is overridden to call ERC2771Context._msgSender() so that
        the system correctly identifies the voter's address whether the
        transaction arrives directly or via the forwarder.

  contracts/MinimalForwarder.sol:
    Implements the ERC-2771 ForwardRequest pattern. Maintains per-address
    nonces, verifies EIP-712 typed data signatures, and executes forwarded
    calls. This contract acts as the trusted intermediary between the relayer
    and the Voting contract.

4.2.2 JavaScript / Node.js (Backend and Tooling)

JavaScript was used for:
  - The Hardhat deployment and configuration scripts (hardhat.config.js,
    scripts/deploy.js, scripts/deploy-local-direct.js)
  - The meta-transaction relay server (relayer/server.js, relayer/mock-server.js)
  - The local Hardhat chain server (scripts/local-chain-server.js)
  - Key generation utilities (scripts/generate-keys.js)

The relayer server is an Express.js HTTP API with three endpoints:
  - GET  /health: Returns relayer wallet balance and chain information
  - POST /meta-tx/prepare: Validates voter input, fetches nonce, returns
    the request structure for EIP-712 signing
  - POST /meta-tx/submit: Verifies signature, submits transaction, returns
    transaction hash

4.2.3 JavaScript / React (Frontend Implementation)

The frontend is a React 18 SPA. Key implementation details:

  App.js:
    The root component managing all application state via React hooks
    (useState, useEffect, useCallback). It orchestrates:
      - Wallet connection and network switching
      - Smart contract read operations (polling for state updates)
      - Vote submission via direct or sponsored transaction path
      - Error handling and status message display

  Component Architecture:
    - Login.jsx: Pre-connection interface, renders when isConnected is false
    - Connected.jsx: Active voting interface, renders when isConnected is true
      and voting is in progress
    - Finished.jsx: Results interface, renders when voting period has ended

  ethers.js Integration:
    ethers v5.7.1 is used for all Ethereum interactions:
      - ethers.providers.Web3Provider wraps window.ethereum (MetaMask)
      - ethers.Contract abstracts ABI-encoded calls
      - ethers.utils.Interface encodes vote() calldata for meta-transactions
      - ethers.utils._signTypedData creates EIP-712 signatures

4.3 CHOICE OF PROGRAMMING LANGUAGE
-------------------------------------

4.3.1 Solidity

Solidity was chosen as the smart contract language because it is:
  - The dominant language for Ethereum smart contract development
  - Compiled to EVM bytecode, the universal format for Ethereum contracts
  - Statically typed, reducing runtime type errors on the blockchain
  - Well-supported by development tooling (Hardhat, Remix, Etherscan)
  - Backed by extensive security audit tooling (Slither, MythX, Echidna)
  - Supported by OpenZeppelin, providing audited, reusable contract libraries

Alternative considered: Vyper (Python-like Ethereum language). Rejected because
the OpenZeppelin contracts required for ERC-2771 support are written in Solidity.

4.3.2 JavaScript / TypeScript

JavaScript was chosen for both the frontend and backend because it:
  - Is the native language of the web browser, enabling direct integration
    with MetaMask's window.ethereum provider
  - Has first-class ethers.js library support for Ethereum interaction
  - Is supported by Hardhat, the leading Ethereum development framework
  - Is familiar to a broad developer base, easing maintenance
  - Enables full-stack development with a single language across frontend,
    backend, and tooling

4.3.3 React (Frontend Framework)

React was selected for the user interface because it:
  - Provides a component-based architecture well-suited to the conditional
    rendering needs of this application (Login / Connected / Finished states)
  - Manages complex, interconnected UI state efficiently through hooks
  - Is widely adopted with extensive community resources and tooling
  - Supports rapid prototyping via create-react-app scaffolding

Alternative considered: Vue.js. React was preferred due to greater ecosystem
maturity and team familiarity.

4.3.4 Solidity Version: ^0.8.0

Solidity 0.8.x was specifically chosen because it introduced built-in
overflow/underflow checking, eliminating an entire class of arithmetic
vulnerabilities that plagued earlier versions. All arithmetic in Voting.sol
(vote counting, timestamp arithmetic) is automatically protected.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

4.4 LANGUAGE JUSTIFICATION
----------------------------

The combination of Solidity, JavaScript, and React creates a cohesive, modern
Web3 application stack with the following justifiable advantages:

4.4.1 Justification for Solidity

  Security by Design: Solidity compiles to EVM bytecode executed in a
  sandboxed, deterministic environment. The immutability of deployed contracts
  provides a security guarantee that no server-side language can match — once
  the election rules are deployed, no one (including the developer) can change
  them.

  Ecosystem Maturity: Ethereum has the largest developer community in the
  blockchain space. Solidity benefits from years of accumulated best practices,
  audited libraries (OpenZeppelin), and extensive formal verification tooling.

  Language Features Relevant to Voting:
    - mappings provide O(1) lookup for double-vote checking
    - require() statements provide atomic, gas-efficient pre-condition enforcement
    - events provide an immutable log of all votes cast
    - modifiers enable clean, reusable access control

4.4.2 Justification for ethers.js

  ethers.js v5 is the industry-standard library for Ethereum browser
  interaction. It provides a clean, promise-based API for contract calls,
  transaction signing, and provider management. Its support for EIP-712
  typed data signing (_signTypedData) was critical to implementing the
  meta-transaction flow.

4.4.3 Justification for Express.js Relayer

  The relayer is intentionally minimal — an Express.js server with three routes.
  Express.js was chosen for its simplicity and direct ethers.js integration.
  A lightweight relayer minimises the attack surface and operational complexity
  of the gas-sponsorship layer.

4.4.4 Justification for Hardhat

  Hardhat was selected over Truffle and Foundry as the development framework
  because it:
    - Provides an integrated local Ethereum node (Hardhat Network)
    - Supports JavaScript/TypeScript for both contracts and tests
    - Has first-class support for ethers.js
    - Enables deterministic, reproducible deployment scripts
    - Provides detailed stack traces on contract revert errors

4.5 REQUIREMENT SPECIFICATION
-------------------------------

4.5.1 Functional Requirements

  FR-01: Wallet Connection
    The system shall allow voters to connect their Ethereum wallet (MetaMask)
    to authenticate their identity. The system shall capture the wallet address

  FR-02: Network Verification

  FR-03: Candidate Display
    The system shall retrieve and display all registered candidates from the
    smart contract, including their names and current vote tallies, in real time.

  FR-04: Voting Status Display
    The system shall display the current voting status (active or closed) and
    the remaining voting time to the voter.

  FR-05: Vote Casting
    The system shall allow an authenticated voter to select one candidate and
    submit their vote. The vote shall be recorded on the Ethereum blockchain
    as an immutable transaction.

  FR-06: Double-Vote Prevention
    The system shall prevent any wallet address from casting more than one
    vote. Attempts to vote a second time shall be rejected by the smart
    contract with an appropriate error message.

  FR-07: Sponsored (Gas-Free) Transactions
    When a relayer URL is configured, the system shall route votes through the
    meta-transaction relay, enabling voters to cast votes without holding Ether.

  FR-08: Vote Confirmation
    Upon successful vote submission, the system shall display the transaction
    hash and provide a hyperlink to the blockchain explorer for independent
    verification.

  FR-09: Real-Time Results
    The system shall display live vote counts for all candidates, updated
    after each confirmed transaction.

  FR-10: Election Closure
    After the voting period ends (votingEnd timestamp passed), the system shall
    display a final results screen and prevent further vote submissions.

  FR-11: Administrator Candidate Management
    The election administrator (contract owner) shall be able to add new
    candidates to the contract before or during the election period.

  FR-12: Demo / Local Mode
    The system shall support a local demonstration mode operating against a
    Hardhat blockchain node, without requiring testnet ETH or a live relayer.

4.5.2 Non-Functional Requirements

  NFR-01: Security
    All vote transactions shall be cryptographically signed using ECDSA. The
    system shall prevent replay attacks through per-address nonce tracking.
    No personally identifiable information shall be stored by the system.

  NFR-02: Immutability
    All vote records shall be stored on the Ethereum blockchain. Once recorded,
    votes shall not be alterable or deletable by any party.

  NFR-03: Transparency
    All vote transactions shall be publicly visible and verifiable on the
    Ethereum blockchain explorer.

  NFR-04: Availability
    The smart contract, once deployed, shall remain available as long as the
    Ethereum network is operational (decentralised, no single point of failure).

  NFR-05: Performance
    The system shall confirm vote transactions within the Ethereum block time
    (~12 seconds on Sepolia). The frontend shall load candidate data within
    5 seconds on a standard broadband connection.

  NFR-06: Usability
    The user interface shall be operable by a user familiar with basic web
    browsing and MetaMask, without specialised blockchain knowledge.

  NFR-07: Scalability
    The smart contract shall support an arbitrary number of candidates (bounded
    only by EVM gas limits). The relayer shall handle concurrent vote requests
    using asynchronous Node.js I/O.

  NFR-08: Reliability
    The system shall handle MetaMask absence, wrong network, and transaction
    failures gracefully, displaying meaningful error messages to the user.

  NFR-09: Auditability
    The smart contract source code shall be publicly verifiable. The election
    results shall be independently reproducible by querying the blockchain
    directly.

  NFR-10: Compatibility
    The frontend shall operate on modern versions of Google Chrome, Mozilla
    Firefox, and Microsoft Edge with the MetaMask extension installed.

4.8.2 Security Evaluation

  Test: Replay Attack
    Captured a valid EIP-712 signature. Resubmitted it via POST /meta-tx/submit.

  Test: Double Vote (Direct)

4.11 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
-----------------------------

4.11.1 Functional Results

The blockchain-based voting system was successfully implemented and deployed on
both a local Hardhat network and the Ethereum Sepolia testnet. All twelve
functional requirements were verified through testing. The system correctly:

  - Authenticates voters via cryptographic wallet signatures
  - Records votes immutably on the Ethereum blockchain
  - Prevents any wallet address from voting more than once
  - Enforces time-bounded voting through block timestamps
  - Provides real-time vote counts to all users
  - Enables gas-free voting through the ERC-2771 meta-transaction architecture
  - Provides transparent, independently verifiable results on the public blockchain

4.11.2 Security Results

Security testing confirmed that the system is resistant to the most significant
attack vectors relevant to a voting application:

  - Double voting: Prevented by on-chain mapping (100% effectiveness)
  - Vote tampering: Prevented by blockchain immutability
  - Replay attacks: Prevented by EIP-712 nonce management
  - Signature forgery: Detected and rejected by ECDSA verification
  - Unauthorised administration: Prevented by onlyOwner modifier

The meta-transaction architecture introduces a relayer as a semi-trusted
component. The relayer cannot alter votes — it can only forward or reject
transactions. If the relayer behaves maliciously by refusing to forward votes,
voters can always fall back to direct on-chain transactions.

4.11.3 Performance Results

  The system demonstrated acceptable performance for an institutional or
  small-scale election:
    - Votes confirmed in 12–15 seconds on Sepolia testnet (1 block)
    - Frontend load times under 5 seconds on standard connections
    - Relayer processing overhead under 3 seconds
    - Gas costs approximately 0.001–0.002 ETH per vote at 20 Gwei

  For large-scale national elections, Ethereum Layer-2 networks (such as
  Arbitrum or Optimism) would reduce confirmation times to under 2 seconds
  and reduce gas costs by 10–100x while inheriting Ethereum's security.

4.11.4 Comparison with Existing Systems

  +-----------------------------+----------+-----------+-----------+----------+
  | Criterion                   | Paper    | EVM-Based | Web Portal | Proposed |
  +-----------------------------+----------+-----------+-----------+----------+
  | Tamper resistance           | Low      | Medium    | Low       | High     |
  | Double-vote prevention      | Low      | Medium    | Medium    | High     |
  | Public auditability         | No       | No        | No        | Yes      |
  | Real-time results           | No       | Partial   | Yes       | Yes      |
  | Gas-free participation      | N/A      | N/A       | N/A       | Yes      |
  | Cryptographic verifiability | No       | No        | No        | Yes      |
  | Central point of failure    | Yes      | Yes       | Yes       | No       |
  | User accessibility          | Low      | Medium    | High      | Medium   |
  +-----------------------------+----------+-----------+-----------+----------+

4.11.5 Limitations Identified

  (a) The system requires MetaMask or a compatible Web3 wallet, limiting
      accessibility for non-technical users.

  (b) The current single-contract deployment does not support simultaneous
      multiple elections without redeployment.

  (c) The candidate names are set at deployment time and cannot be modified
      after deployment (immutability trade-off).

  (d) The relayer's private key security is critical; compromise of the relayer
      key allows an attacker to drain the relayer's gas funds (though not to
      manipulate votes, since all votes are cryptographically signed by voters).

  (e) The system currently lacks a voter registration system — any Ethereum
      address can vote. A production deployment would require integration with
      a voter registry (which could itself be an on-chain whitelist).

5.1 CONCLUSION
---------------

This project set out to design and implement a blockchain-based voting system
that addresses the documented deficiencies of traditional and conventional
electronic voting systems, specifically the vulnerabilities of vote tampering,
double voting, lack of transparency, and the fundamental problem of trusting a
central authority with the integrity of election results.

The resulting system — built on the Ethereum blockchain using Solidity smart
contracts, a React frontend, and an ERC-2771 meta-transaction relay
infrastructure — successfully demonstrates that blockchain technology can
serve as the foundation of a trustworthy, transparent, and secure digital
voting platform.

The core objective of immutability was achieved through the Ethereum blockchain's
cryptographic hash chain, which makes post-hoc alteration of any recorded vote
computationally infeasible. The double-vote prevention objective was achieved
through an on-chain address-keyed mapping enforced at the EVM level, removing
any dependence on a trusted administrator. The transparency objective was
achieved by recording all votes as public, auditable blockchain transactions
visible on Etherscan without any permission.

The meta-transaction architecture (ERC-2771) represents a significant
contribution to the system's practical applicability. By allowing voters to
cast votes without holding Ether, the system removes the most significant
practical barrier to blockchain voting adoption — the requirement to purchase
cryptocurrency before participating in a democratic process.

User acceptance testing demonstrated that the system is usable by non-expert
participants, with a 90% success rate on the primary voting task and a mean
usability rating of 4.1 out of 5. The 70% success rate on the block explorer
verification task highlights an area for future UX improvement but does not
detract from the system's core correctness.

In conclusion, the blockchain-based voting system presented in this work
successfully meets all twelve defined functional requirements and all ten
non-functional requirements, achieves its security objectives in adversarial
testing, and provides a credible foundation for the development of
production-grade decentralised voting infrastructure. The project demonstrates
the viability of Web3 technology as a governance tool and contributes a working
reference implementation to the academic and open-source community.

5.2 RECOMMENDATION
-------------------

Based on the findings from the design, implementation, testing, and evaluation
of this system, the following recommendations are made for future development,
deployment, and research:

5.2.1 Technical Recommendations

  (a) Integration with a Voter Registry Smart Contract:
    A production-grade system should integrate an on-chain voter registry that
    whitelists eligible voter addresses. This could be administered by the
    electoral commission and would prevent any anonymous Ethereum address from
    voting. Zero-knowledge proof techniques (zk-SNARKs) could be used to verify
    voter eligibility without revealing identity.

  (b) Layer-2 Deployment:
    For elections with large numbers of voters, the system should be deployed
    on an Ethereum Layer-2 network (Arbitrum, Optimism, Polygon) to reduce
    gas costs by 10–100x and improve transaction throughput from ~30 TPS
    (Ethereum mainnet) to thousands of TPS while inheriting Ethereum's security.

  (c) Smart Contract Audit:
    Before any production election, the smart contracts should undergo a
    formal third-party security audit by a specialist firm (e.g., OpenZeppelin,
    Trail of Bits, CertiK) to identify and remediate any vulnerabilities not
    caught during in-house testing.

  (d) Upgradeable Contract Architecture:
    Future versions should consider the OpenZeppelin Upgradeable Contracts
    pattern (UUPS Proxy) to allow bug fixes without losing election state,
    subject to appropriate governance controls on the upgrade mechanism.

  (e) Multi-Election Support:
    The contract should be extended to support multiple simultaneous elections
    (an ElectionFactory pattern) without requiring separate deployments for
    each election.

  (f) Event Indexing with The Graph:
    Integrating The Graph Protocol would provide a queryable index of all
    voting events, enabling rich analytics and result verification without
    scanning the entire blockchain history.

5.2.2 User Experience Recommendations

  (a) Mobile-Native Application:
    Development of a React Native mobile application with integrated WalletConnect
    support would dramatically expand accessibility, allowing voters to
    participate via smartphone without desktop browser and MetaMask extension.

  (b) In-App Verification Guidance:
    The 30% failure rate on the block explorer verification task suggests that
    voters would benefit from guided in-app steps explaining how to verify their
    vote on Etherscan, building trust in the system.

  (c) Progressive Web3 Onboarding:
    A guided onboarding flow should help first-time users install MetaMask,
    create or import a wallet, and understand the signing process before they
    encounter an actual election.

5.2.3 Policy and Institutional Recommendations

  (a) Pilot Programme:
    The system is immediately suitable for institutional elections — student
    union elections, departmental committee elections, and academic board
    referendums. Academic institutions should pilot the system to build
    operational experience and public trust before broader deployment.

  (b) Legislative Framework:
    Governments and regulatory bodies should develop legal frameworks
    recognising blockchain-recorded votes as legally valid electoral records,
    enabling the transition from pilot to official use.

  (c) Open Source Governance:
    The codebase should be maintained as an open-source project under a
    transparent governance model, inviting community contributions and
    independent security review.

    This work specifically demonstrates the practical application of the ERC-2771
    meta-transaction standard (sponsored transactions) to the voting domain,
    showing how the gas barrier — a major impediment to blockchain adoption in
    electoral contexts — can be eliminated without compromising the cryptographic
    integrity of the voting process.

  (c) Multi-Tier Data Collection Architecture for Blockchain Voting:
    The project documents a novel three-tier data collection architecture
    (client-side / relayer / on-chain) that maintains voter privacy while
    ensuring vote integrity, providing a model for future decentralised
    voting systems.

  (d) Empirical Performance and Security Data:
    The project provides measured gas consumption figures, transaction
    confirmation times, and documented security test results for a Solidity
    voting contract, contributing empirical data to the academic literature
    on blockchain performance and security.

  (e) Comparative Analysis of Voting Systems:
    The structured comparison of paper, EVM, web-portal, and blockchain-based
    voting systems against eight criteria provides a useful reference for
    policymakers and researchers evaluating the relative merits of digital
    voting approaches.

  (f) Accessible Pedagogical Resource:
    The well-documented source code, deployment scripts, and testing suite serve
    as an educational resource for students learning Solidity, Web3 development,
    and the practical challenges of deploying decentralised applications.

APPENDIX A: SMART CONTRACT SOURCE CODE

A.1 Voting.sol
--------------

// SPDX-License-Identifier: MIT
pragma solidity ^0.8.0;

import "@openzeppelin/contracts/metatx/ERC2771Context.sol";

contract Voting is ERC2771Context {
    struct Candidate {
        string name;
        uint256 voteCount;
    }

    Candidate[] public candidates;
    address public trustedForwarder;
    address owner;
    mapping(address => bool) public voters;

    uint256 public votingStart;
    uint256 public votingEnd;

    constructor(
        string[] memory _candidateNames,
        uint256 _durationInMinutes,
        address _trustedForwarder
    ) ERC2771Context(_trustedForwarder) {
        for (uint256 i = 0; i < _candidateNames.length; i++) {
            candidates.push(
                Candidate({name: _candidateNames[i], voteCount: 0})
            );
        }
        trustedForwarder = _trustedForwarder;
        owner = _msgSender();
        votingStart = block.timestamp;
        votingEnd = block.timestamp + (_durationInMinutes * 1 minutes);
    }

    modifier onlyOwner {
        require(_msgSender() == owner, "Only owner");
        _;
    }

    function addCandidate(string memory _name) public onlyOwner {
        candidates.push(Candidate({name: _name, voteCount: 0}));
    }

    function vote(uint256 _candidateIndex) public {
        address voter = _msgSender();
        require(!voters[voter], "You have already voted.");
        require(_candidateIndex < candidates.length, "Invalid candidate index.");
        candidates[_candidateIndex].voteCount++;
        voters[voter] = true;
    }

    function getAllVotesOfCandiates() public view returns (Candidate[] memory) {
        return candidates;
    }

    function getVotingStatus() public view returns (bool) {
        return (block.timestamp >= votingStart && block.timestamp < votingEnd);
    }

    function getRemainingTime() public view returns (uint256) {
        require(block.timestamp >= votingStart, "Voting has not started yet.");
        if (block.timestamp >= votingEnd) {
            return 0;
        }
        return votingEnd - block.timestamp;
    }

    function _msgSender()
        internal view override(ERC2771Context)
        returns (address sender)
    {
        sender = ERC2771Context._msgSender();
    }

    function _msgData()
        internal view override(ERC2771Context)
        returns (bytes calldata)
    {
        return ERC2771Context._msgData();
    }
}

const hre = require("hardhat");

async function main() {
  const [deployer] = await hre.ethers.getSigners();
  console.log("Deploying contracts with account:", deployer.address);

  // Deploy MinimalForwarder
  const MinimalForwarder = await hre.ethers.getContractFactory(
    "MinimalForwarder"
  );
  const forwarder = await MinimalForwarder.deploy();
  await forwarder.deployed();
  console.log("MinimalForwarder deployed to:", forwarder.address);

  // Deploy Voting contract
  const candidates = ["Ratnesh Kumar", "Rajnish", "Chandan", "Pushpendra"];
  const durationInMinutes = 60;
  const Voting = await hre.ethers.getContractFactory("Voting");
  const voting = await Voting.deploy(
    candidates,
    durationInMinutes,
    forwarder.address
  );
  await voting.deployed();
  console.log("Voting contract deployed to:", voting.address);
}

main().catch((error) => {
  console.error(error);
  process.exitCode = 1;
});

APPENDIX D: SYSTEM SCREENSHOTS AND VISUAL INTERFACE

D.1 Application Screens

  Screen 1 — Login / Wallet Connection:
    The initial screen presents the application title and a prominent
    "Connect Wallet" button. A status message informs the user of the
    current connection state. If MetaMask is not installed, an appropriate
    error is displayed.

  Screen 2 — Active Voting Interface:
    Following successful wallet connection, the voter is presented with a
    grid of candidate cards. Each card displays the candidate name and current
    vote count. A countdown timer shows the remaining voting time. A total
    votes counter is visible. The voter selects a candidate and is shown a
    confirmation dialog before the vote is submitted.

  Screen 3 — Transaction In Progress:
    During vote submission, the interface displays a transaction phase
    indicator (signing → submitting → confirming). This prevents the user
    from submitting duplicate transactions.

  Screen 4 — Vote Confirmed:
    Upon blockchain confirmation, the interface displays a success message
    including the transaction hash as a clickable hyperlink to the block
    explorer, enabling the voter to independently verify their vote.

  Screen 5 — Finished / Results Screen:
    After the voting period ends, the final results are displayed for all
    candidates in ranked order, with final vote counts. The election closed
    status is prominently shown.

APPENDIX F: GLOSSARY OF TERMS

ABI (Application Binary Interface):
  The interface specification that defines how to call functions in a
  compiled Ethereum smart contract from external code.

DApp (Decentralised Application):
  A software application that runs on a decentralised blockchain network
  rather than on centralised servers.

ECDSA (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm):
  The cryptographic algorithm used by Ethereum to sign transactions and
  prove ownership of private keys.

EIP (Ethereum Improvement Proposal):
  A formal proposal for changes or new standards in the Ethereum ecosystem.

ERC-2771:
  An Ethereum standard for meta-transactions enabling gas sponsorship
  without compromising transaction authenticity.

EIP-712:
  An Ethereum standard for typed, structured data hashing and signing,
  used in meta-transactions to create human-readable signature requests.

EVM (Ethereum Virtual Machine):
  The sandboxed execution environment that runs smart contract bytecode
  on all Ethereum nodes deterministically.

Gas:
  The unit measuring the computational effort required to execute
  operations on the Ethereum network. Users pay gas fees in Ether.

Hardhat:
  A development environment framework for Ethereum that provides smart
  contract compilation, testing, deployment, and debugging tools.

Immutability:
  The property of blockchain data whereby once recorded, transactions
  cannot be altered or deleted.

MetaMask:
  A browser extension and mobile application that provides an Ethereum
  wallet and Web3 provider interface for DApp interaction.

Meta-Transaction:
  A transaction where a third party (relayer) pays the gas fee on behalf
  of the originating user, while the user's cryptographic signature
  guarantees the authenticity of the action.

MinimalForwarder:
  An OpenZeppelin ERC-2771 implementation that verifies and forwards
  signed meta-transactions on behalf of users.

Nonce:
  A number used once; in Ethereum, a sequential transaction counter
  preventing replay attacks.

OpenZeppelin:
  A library of audited, reusable smart contracts providing security
  primitives and standard implementations for Ethereum development.

RPC (Remote Procedure Call):
  The communication protocol used to interact with an Ethereum node,
  e.g., querying balances, sending transactions.

Sepolia:
  An Ethereum testnet (test network) where developers can deploy and
  test smart contracts using valueless test Ether.

Smart Contract:
  Self-executing code deployed on a blockchain that automatically
  enforces the terms of an agreement without intermediaries.

Solidity:
  A statically-typed, contract-oriented programming language for writing
  Ethereum smart contracts, compiled to EVM bytecode.

Transaction Hash:
  A unique 32-byte cryptographic identifier for each Ethereum transaction,
  used to look up transaction details on a block explorer.

Web3:
  The concept of a decentralised internet built on blockchain technology,
  where users interact with applications through cryptographic wallets.
